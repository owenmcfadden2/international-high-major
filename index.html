Finding 05

The portal doesn't stop at the border

102/237

Roughly 43% of players in this set already show more than one NCAA program on their record — meaning the transfer portal churn hitting domestic recruits is just as present among international arrivals.

Finding 06

Rebounding and playmaking carry over. Scoring doesn't.

Comparing each player's final pro season to their first NCAA season, per 36 minutes played, across the 130 players with a real season on both sides.

r = 0.54
Rebound rate, pro → NCAA. A strong correlation — the board skill travels.
r = 0.49
Assist rate, pro → NCAA. Playmaking is nearly as sticky as rebounding.
r = −0.06
Scoring rate, pro → NCAA. Essentially no relationship — a high pro scoring rate is close to a coin flip on translating.

Holds after filtering out short, low-minute pro cameos (games ≥ 8, mpg ≥ 10, n = 90): rebounding r = 0.52, assists r = 0.40, scoring r = 0.16 (not significant). Likely explanation: rebounding and passing are closer to raw skill, while pro scoring rate is heavily shaped by role — go-to option on a weak team vs. complementary piece on a deep one — and that role often flips on arrival.

If anything the order runs backwards from what you'd expect — Euroleague players land Power conferences at a lower rate than Domestic Tier 1 or Tier 2 players, and Youth/ANGT-only prospects place about as often as Euroleague ones. League prestige alone doesn't appear to be what separates a Power-conference recruit from a mid-major one; fit, need, relationships, and film likely carry more weight than the name of the league on the scouting report.

Finding 08

Sophomore-year data is thin (n=33) but suggestive.

Only 33 players in this set have a real, minutes-logged second NCAA season so far — mostly from the 2024–25 cohort. Small sample, but the pattern is consistent enough to flag.

r = −0.02 → 0.44
Pro minutes per game vs. NCAA scoring rate: no relationship as a freshman (year 1), but a real one by year 2 (p = 0.012).
0.34 → 0.35
Pro games played vs. NCAA minutes: roughly steady from year 1 to year 2 — the durability signal shows up early and holds.
r = −0.32
Year-1 minutes vs. year-2 minutes growth (p = 0.067). Lower-usage freshmen tend to see the bigger sophomore jump — a fairly normal development curve.
40%

Of the 33 players with a real year 2, 13 (about 40%) had already transferred to a different school by their sophomore season — and those transfers played slightly more, not fewer, freshman minutes on average (13.9 vs. 10.9 mpg) than the players who stayed put. Too small a sample to lean on, but it argues against a simple “struggling freshmen hit the portal” story.

Reading: freshman year looks like a noisy adjustment period where pro track record doesn't cleanly predict NCAA output yet. By year two, once role and rotation have settled, production starts looking more like what a player's pro history would have suggested all along.
